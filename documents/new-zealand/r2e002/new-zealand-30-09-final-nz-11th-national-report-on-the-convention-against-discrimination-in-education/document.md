## 1. Introduction

The Convention against Discrimination in Education ('the  Convention')  and  the  Recommendation  against Discrimination in Education ('the Recommendation'), adopted by UNESCO's General Conference in 1960, are  aligned  with  UNESCO's  constitutional  mandate  to  'advance  the  ideal  of  equality  of  educational opportunities without regard to race, sex or any distinctions, economic or social'. The Convention is the first legally  binding  international  instrument  focused  on  the right  to  education and  the  only  one  exclusively dedicated to it.

The right to education is one of the key principles underpinning the Education 2030 agenda and Sustainable Development Goal 4 (SDG4) adopted by the international community in 2015. SDG 4 seeks to ensure the full enjoyment of the right to education as fundamental to achieving sustainable development. Its scope captures the multidimensional nature of the right to education.

Representing a powerful tool to advance SDG 4 by ensuring effective articulation between normative and operational work, the Convention has been recognized as a cornerstone of Education 2030 agenda.

The present Guidelines are intended to assist Member States in the preparation of country reports on the implementation of the Convention and the Recommendation in the context of the 11th consultation.

## 2. Scope of the Consultation

Considering the Education 2030 agenda, the monitoring of the Convention and the Recommendation is of crucial importance to ensure an education that is truly inclusive for all and to counter persistent inequalities in access, participation and learning outcomes at all levels, particularly for the most vulnerable groups. This is particularly true under the current context in which countries are making efforts to recover from the learning disruption caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. Concrete measures taken by countries in accordance with their legal obligations under these instruments contribute to implementing the Education 2030 agenda.

As the scope and content of the legal obligations under the right to education are aligned with the 10 targets of SDG 4, efforts are being made to ensure that the components of the targets are included in the guidelines and that these guidelines serve as a valuable contribution to monitoring progress towards SDG 4 targets.

Beyond SDG 4, the Convention is also closely aligned with the normative content on the right to education of other international human rights instruments such as the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (1966), Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (1979), the Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989) and the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006).

In addition, this Consultation is an opportunity to inform the ongoing UNESCO Initiative on the Evolving Right to Education within a lifelong learning perspective 1  (launched in December 2021). The Initiative investigates how the right to education could be reinforced placing it within a lifelong learning perspective and in  light  of  the  new  demands  being  placed  on  education.  This  Initiative  builds  on  the Report  from  the International Commission on the Futures of Education 2 , which suggests to broaden our understanding of the right to education to clearly apply it to all ages and across all times and spaces. The initiative is also in-line with the Vision Statement of the United Nations Secretary General on Transforming Education 3 , and the Report  on  the  2022  Transforming  Education  Summit 4 which  call  for  the  transformation  of  education systems  to  'include  everyone,  regardless  of  circumstance,  context  or  background'.  The  Initiative  on  the Evolving Right to Education aims to open a collaborative and participatory discussion about the international framework to ensure the effective enforcement of the right to education and learning throughout life for all. Finally, the Initiative builds on recent inter-governmental dialogues, including the Marrakech Framework for Action  for  Adult  Learning  and  Education 5  and  the  Tashkent  Declaration  on  Early  Childood  Care  and Education. 6

## 3. Reporting obligations

There are differences in the legal nature of the obligations under the Convention, as distinguished from those under the Recommendation. The Convention has binding force and the States Parties are under the obligation to incorporate its provisions into their domestic frameworks. The Recommendation, non-binding in nature, has political  and  moral  force.  Its  adoption  sought  to  take  into  account  the  difficulties  that  certain  States  might experience in ratifying the Convention, in particular with respect to their federal structure.

Bearing in mind the differences in legal scope inherent to the nature of these two categories of instruments, the content of the Recommendation is identical to that of the Convention. Member States are expected to give effect to it in national legislation and education policies.

The reporting obligation under the provisions of Article VII of the Convention and of the Recommendation is the  same.  Article  VIII  of  UNESCO's  Constitution  states  that  ' Each  Member  State  shall  submit  to  the Organization, at such times and in such manner as shall be determined by the General Conference, reports on the laws, regulations and statistics relating to its educational, scientific and cultural institutions and activities, and on the action taken upon the recommendations and conventions referred to in Article IV, paragraph 4' . Therefore,  even  if  a  country  is  not  a  State  Party  to  the  Convention,  it  needs  to  submit  a  report  on  the implementation of the Recommendation, which means that all UNESCO Member States are expected to submit  a  national  report on  the  implementation  of  these  instruments  in  the  context  of  monitoring consultations.

## 4. Participation in consultations

The  reporting period for the 11th consultation on  the  implementation  of  the  Convention  and  the Recommendation covers four years (2021-2024).

The Futures of Education Report calls for a new social contract for education, which requires to understand education  as  a  common  good  and  a  shared  societal  endeavour.  To  ensure  this,  extensive  consultations involving a broad range of stakeholders is needed. The reporting process therefore needs to be participatory. This includes ensuring cooperation between ministries of education and other ministries dealing with aspects of  the  right  to  education  as  well  as  consulting  social  partners  and civil  society  organizations and  other relevant bodies.

The  preparation  of  national  reports  provides  a good  opportunity for  Member  States  to  assess  the implementation of the right to education in their country, identify specific challenges, build constructive dialogue with other actors involved in this field and share good practices.

## 5. Modalities and structure

The Guidelines are structured around three chapters:

-The first chapter covers general information on adherence to the Convention.

-The second  chapter aims  to  gather  information  on  the  implementation  of  the  Convention  and Recommendation in the context of Sustainable Development Goal 4. As the most extensive part of the Guidelines, this chapter covers: inclusion and non-discrimination in education (section 1), all levels and forms of education (section 2), quality education provision and learning environment (section 3) and finally, strengthening governance systems (section 4).

-The third chapter seeks to obtain information on the methods used to draw the attention of the various authorities in the country to the instruments.

With a view to simplify the Guidelines while providing necessary substantive information to assist Member States in the preparation of responses, through a participatory process, two types of guidance are provided:

- Open-ended and closed-ended questions to be directly filled in by Member States. Please note that multiple answers may be provided where relevant.

- Additional information aimed at guiding Members States to structure and detail their responses to the open-ended questions (including remarks on how to respond efficiently to the questions raised). These elements are presented in italics.

Information  could  also  be  given  about  the  judicial,  administrative  and  other  competent  authorities  having jurisdiction with respect to the right to education.

The  Education  and  Training  Act  2020  establishes  a  right  to  education  that  is  not  a  freestanding  one enforceable through the Courts. Under this Act, children aged 5 to 19 have a right to free education, and children with special education needs have the same rights to enrol, attend and receive education at a State school as all other children. If the Secretary for Education is satisfied that a student requires an educational service beyond that normally obtained in an ordinary class in a State school, that student can enrol in a specialist school or receive help from a special service under Section 37 of the Act. The application the Education and Training Act 2020 receives external scrutiny through the Education Review Office's review processes,  by  the  Ombudsman,  the  Controller  and  Auditor  General,  parliamentary  processes  and international review.

New Zealand jurisprudence about the right to education includes Daniels v The Attorney-General 11 . In the High Court (2002), Justice Baragnawath said that 'Where a child's needs are met in an ordinary school, the Crown's duty in respect of that child has been discharged  where a conventional school is incapable of meeting a child's need, the Crown has an obligation, pursuant to s. 9 of the Education Act 1989, to provide special education to the child sufficient to enable the child to receive some sort of worthwhile education.' However, the Court of Appeal (2003) found that the statutory right to education, and the corresponding legal obligation  on  education  authorities  to  meet  the  education  needs  of  each  individual,  apply  equally  to  all children irrespective of whether they have special needs or not. In the view of the Court of Appeal the right to a suitable education was promoted by the establishment of a system by which it was provided, rather than in its being a judicially enforceable right.

#### Human Rights Act 1993

Under  this  Act,  discrimination  in  access  to  educational  establishments  is  prohibited  (section  57).  The prohibited grounds of discrimination are included within section 21.

Under the Human Rights Act, (subject to some exceptions), it is unlawful for any educational establishment, governance body, management or teacher to discriminate against a person where so doing would result in:

- The refusal or failure to admit a person as a pupil or student; or

- The admittance of a person as a pupil or a student on less favourable terms and conditions than would otherwise be made available; or

- The denial or restricted access to any benefits or services provided by the establishment; or

- The exclusion of a person as a pupil or a student or subject him or her to any other detriment.

The prohibited grounds for discrimination are:

- Sex including pregnancy and childbirth, sexual orientation

- Marital or family status (including gender identity)

- Religious and ethical beliefs

- Colour, race, ethnic or national origins

- Disability

- Age

- Political opinion

- Employment status

- Family status.

- Sexual orientation.

The Human Rights Act permits measures to be taken in good faith for the purpose of assisting or advancing persons or groups of persons disadvantaged because of discrimination to achieve an equal place with other members of the community .

#### Bill of Rights Act 1990

This Act sets out the right to freedom from discrimination on the grounds of discrimination as detailed in the Human Rights Act 1993 (section 19).

#### Te Tiriti o Waitangi/The Treaty of Waitangi

As a partner to Te Tiriti o Waitangi/the Treaty of Waitangi (Te Tiriti/the Treaty), the Crown has a duty to actively protect Māori rights and interests including taonga (treasure). For the education system this duty is recognised through several provisions in the Education and Training Act 2020.  Some notable provisions relate to sections 4, 6, 9 and 127:

- Section 4(d) - states that one of the purposes of the Education and Training Act 2020 is to establish and  regulate  an  education  system  that  honours  Te  Tiriti/the  Treaty  and  supports  Māori-Crown relationships.

- Section 6 - the Minister of Education and the Minister of Māori-Crown Relations: Te Arawhiti, may, for the purposes of providing equitable outcomes for all students, jointly issue a statement that provides greater specificity around what education agencies must do to give effect to the public service objectives that relate to Te Tiriti/the Treaty. Ministers have not yet determined a need to issue this joint statement.

- Section 9 - sets out all the key Te Tiriti/the Treaty-related provisions within the Education and Training Act 2020.

- Section 127 - relates to school boards who are the employer of all staff and oversee the governance of state and state-integrated schools in New Zealand. It states that one of a board's primary objectives in governing a school is to ensure that the school gives effect to Te Tiriti/the Treaty by:

- o working to ensure that its plans, policies, and local curriculum reflect local tikanga Māori (customary practice), mātauranga Māori (knowledge), and te ao Māori (Māori language, practices, customs and knowledge); and

#### The Education and Training Act 2020

The  Education  and  Training  Act  2020  is  New  Zealand's  primary  education  legislation.  It  ensures  that education  in  New  Zealand  schools  is  non-discriminatory,  inclusive,  culturally,  and  physically  safe  for students, and reflects the importance of Te Tiriti/the Treaty.

The Education and Training Act 2020 came into effect on 1 August 2020. It incorporated and replaced the Education  Acts  1964  and  1989  and  implemented  changes  to  strengthen  provisions  to  eradicate discrimination, racism, and bias within the education sector. Changes included:

- The enabling provision for the Ministers of Education and Māori-Crown Relationships: Te Arawhiti to issue  a  statement  of  expectations  of  education  agencies  with  the  purpose  of  achieving  equitable outcomes for all students (mentioned above).

- A provision that explicitly states that students have the right to attend the school at which they are enrolled for all the hours it is open.

- Requiring parents to confirm in writing if they wish their child to attend or take part in any religious instruction. This changes the onus from the Education Act 1964 whereby parents must opt out of such instruction.

- Establishing the legislative framework for a disputes resolution scheme for students to facilitate and promote  the  resolution  of  serious  disputes  between  students  and  State  schools.  Serious  disputes include any racism or other form of discrimination experienced by the student while at the school.

- Amending objectives for school boards which include a requirement that boards give effect to relevant student rights set out in the new legislation, the New Zealand Bill of Rights Act 1990, and the Human Rights Act 1993.

- Amending school board objectives to require the board to:

- o take all reasonable steps to eliminate racism, stigma, bullying, and any other forms of discrimination within the school.

- o give effect to Te Tiriti/the Treaty:

- Working to ensure that its plans, policies and local curriculum reflect local tikanga Māori, mātauranga Māori, and te ao Māori; and

- Taking all reasonable steps to make instruction available in tikanga Māori and te reo Māori; and

- Achieving equitable outcomes for Māori students (mentioned above).

Section 33 of the Education and Training Act 2020 provides that every domestic student is entitled to free enrolment and free education at any State school during the period beginning on the student's fifth birthday and  ending  on  1 January  after  the  student's  19th  birthday.  Within  this  period,  attendance  at  school  is compulsory from a person's 6th birthday ending on their 16th birthday. 16

Section 34 of the Education and Training Act 2020 clarifies that students with special education needs have the same rights to enrol, attend and receive education at a state school during all the hours the school is open as those who do not. These rights apply to all domestic students, irrespective of their national origins.

There are some restrictions in the legislation on attendance at certain schools. For example, the Minister may declare any school to be a boys' school, a girls' school, or a coeducational school.

School  Boards  of  Trustees  (the  governance  structure  for  State  schools)  must  ensure  their  school  is  a physically and emotionally safe place for all students and staff. #### Education (Pastoral Care of Tertiary and International Learners) Code of Practice 2021 (the Code)

The Education (Pastoral Care of Tertiary and International Learners) Code of Practice 2021 makes sure that  tertiary  and  international  learners  enrolled  with  New  Zealand  education  providers  are  safe  and supported. The Code sets out what education providers must do to ensure the wellbeing and safety of their learners. The Code ensures that providers understand and respond to diverse learner voices and wellbeing and  safety  needs  in  a  way  that  upholds  their  mana  and  autonomy,  and  that  providers  foster  learning environments that are safe and designed to support positive learning experiences of diverse learner groups. The Code also outlines that providers must have practices for:

- Reducing harm to learners resulting from discrimination, racism (including systemic racism), bullying, harassment and abuse

- Working with learners and staff to recognise and respond effectively to discrimination racism (including systemic racism), bullying, harassment and abuse

- Promoting an inclusive culture across the learning environment

- Upholding the cultural needs and aspirations of all groups throughout the learning environment

For more information, see: https://www.education.govt.nz/news/new-code-of-practice-for-the-pastoral-careof-tertiary-and-international-learners/.

#### National Curricula

New  Zealand  has  two  curricula  for  schooling  The  New  Zealand  Curriculum and Te  Marautanga  o Aotearoa . The New Zealand Curriculum acknowledges New Zealand's official languages - te reo Māori and New Zealand Sign Language, as well as English. Te Marautanga o Aotearoa is designed for Māori language education pathways and recognises that every ākonga deserves to be supported to reach their full potential and to be immersed and gain expertise in te reo Māori, tikanga Māori, mātauranga Māori, and te ao Māori (Māori language, practices, customs, and knowledge).

The New Zealand Curriculum is in the process of being refreshed and Te Marautanga o Aotearoa is in the process of being redesigned. While building on the strengths of our current national curriculum, this work aims to establish a knowledge-rich curriculum grounded in the science of learning that is clear about what to teach, and by when, each year of schooling. ##### Policy measures aim to enhance inclusion in education for all

In April 2024, the New Zealand Government announced six education priorities to deliver a world-leading education system that sets all New Zealand children and young people up for success by lifting achievement. These priorities are:

- Clearer curriculum: Establishing a knowledge-rich curriculum grounded in the science of learning.

- Better approach to literacy and numeracy: Implementing evidence-based instruction in early literacy and mathematics.

##### Equity Index

The Equity Index (EQI) is a statistical model that estimates the extent to which students at a school face socio-economic  barriers  to  educational  achievement.  It  was  introduced  in  2023  and  allows  for  more accurately targeted resourcing to support schools with students facing greater socioeconomic disadvantage. For more information, see: https://www.education.govt.nz/our-work/changes-ineducation/equity-index/.

Every year, each school is given an Equity Index number (between 344 and 569). To produce this number, the Equity Index takes into account the circumstances of the individual students actually attending each school and is refreshed each year, so that it stays up to date and continues to reflect changes in each school's community over time.

The EQI is used to support distributing funding to schools that have greater numbers of students experiencing socio-economic barriers to educational achievement. Schools with higher concentrations of students facing socioeconomic barriers receive more funding (called 'Equity Funding'), and they have discretion over how to use this to support their students. The Equity Index has also been used to support targeting funding in other programmes such as the school donations scheme and Ka Ora Ka Ako | Healthy School Lunches Programme.

An  evaluation  is  underway  to  assess  the  influence  of  the  Equity  Index  on  school  practices,  student outcomes and community perceptions of schools. The evaluation is planned to run until 2026. The Equity Index itself will also be improved over time, as more administrative data that can be used in the Equity Index becomes available.

##### School donations scheme

Domestic students in New Zealand are entitled to free enrolment and education. However, schools may also seek voluntary donations from their students' families, caregivers, and communities for general or specific purposes. The school donations scheme was introduced in 2019 and is set out in Section 551 of the Education and Training Act 2020. The scheme is designed to alleviate the pressure and expectations on families, caregivers, and communities most likely to face financial pressures to pay school donations.

State and state integrated schools with an EQI value of 432 or higher will be able to opt into the scheme, reflecting the top 70% of schools whose students face the greatest barriers to achieving in education. Eligible schools may opt into the scheme and receive a pre-student grant payment on the condition that they do not ask for voluntary school donations from their students' parents and caregivers (except for overnight camps).

Ka Ora Ka Ako | Healthy School Lunches Programme aims to reduce food insecurity by providing access to food every school day to all learners in eligible schools. Ka Ora Ka Ako is targeted at the top 25% of schools and kura with learners facing the greatest socio-economic barriers according to the Equity Index. #### Private schools

Private schools are governed by their own independent boards and must be registered with the Ministry of Education under section 214 of the Education and Training Act 2020. Schedule 7 of the Education and Training Act 2020 sets out the legislative requirements for private schools.

Private schools have considerable freedom in the curriculum they offer, how it is delivered, and their school operating hours. Private schools may charge for school fees and other associated costs, and they also receive a private school subsidy from the government which is determined by a formula. Private schools can be primary, intermediate or secondary.

#### Homeschooling

Homeschooling is when parents educate their children themselves, instead of enrolling them in a school. Parents  who want to educate their  children  at  home  need  to  apply  to  the  Ministry  of  Education  for  a Certificate of Exemption from enrolment at a registered school. The Ministry of Education must be satisfied that the child will be taught at least as regularly and as well as they would be in a registered school.

New  Zealand's  further  education  providers  are  largely  publicly  funded.  However,  private  training establishments (PTEs) may be established and registered under the Education and Training Act 2020. PTEs are operated by a wide range of companies, trusts and other entities, and offer post-school education or vocational training. These are diverse in terms of their scale of operation, location, ethnicity, culture and areas of educational expertise.

All private educational institutions, including private schools and PTEs, are subject to the Human Rights Act 1993. With some limited exceptions, private educational institutions are not permitted to discriminate against  students  on  the  grounds  of  sex,  marital  status,  religious  belief,  colour,  race  or  ethnic  origins, disability age, political opinion, employment or family status or sexual orientation, where such a breach would result in:

- The refusal or failure to admit a person as a pupil or student; or

- The admittance of a person as a pupil or a student on less favourable terms and conditions than would otherwise be made available; or

- The denial or restricted access to any benefits or services provided by the establishment; or

- The exclusion of a person as a pupil or a student or subject him or her to any other detriment.

The exceptions are that educational establishments providing services for students of one sex, race, or religious  belief,  a  particular  disability,  or  age  group,  are  not  breaching  the  Act  if  they  refuse  to  admit students of a different sex, race, or religious belief, disability or age. #### Strengthening assessment practices through digital tools

Implementing smarter and consistent assessment is one of the key education priorities of the government.

The  role  of  government  education  agencies  has  been  refocused  to  support education  system  leaders,  such  as  teachers  and  education  providers.  The  government  sets  annual objectives in a Statement of Intent for its central education agencies, and the Ministry of Education develops a national policy framework. Funding aims to support free schooling, and although tertiary study involves costs to the student, financial support is available. A part of the funding that tertiary institutions receive depends on student achievement.

In New Zealand, the school board is the governing body of the school, composed of five elected parent representatives, the principal, a student (secondary schools only) and a teacher representative. The board is the employer of staff and has responsibilities for setting targets and reporting on them, management of school  curriculum,  financial  and  property  management,  health  and  safety  and  policies  and  procedures, student  achievements  and  discipline.  The  board  also  plays  a  key  role  in  supporting  principals  in  their planning, reporting and self-review tasks and in evaluating them. Many of the board's responsibilities are delegated to the principal on a day-to-day basis, but the school board is ultimately collectively responsible for all governance decisions.

The School Trustees Association, funded by the Ministry of Education, provides support services to boards, including principals. The Association employs people with a background in school governance (e.g., former board members) as well as specialists in areas for which boards are responsible (e.g., employment lawyers). It also helps spread best practice via a large annual national conference and in advisory services to schools.

The  tertiary  education  system  is  designed  so  that  there  is  limited  direct  influence  in  the  day-to-day functioning of the Crown agencies that oversee tertiary education and the organisations that deliver it. This protects institutional autonomy and keeps separate the government's purchasing and investment role (TEC), its quality assurance role (NZQA) and its policy, legislative roles (Ministry of Education).

### Wānanga

Wānanga play a unique role in the tertiary education sector because of their 'for Māori, by Māori' approach, commitment to and involvement with the protection and revitalisation of te ao, te reo and mātauranga Māori. The Education and Training Amendment Act 2023 established a new Wānanga Enabling Framework. This Act amends the Education and Training Act 2020, establishing a new 'enabling' framework for the wānanga sector creating new fit-for-purpose administrative settings for wānanga to recognise their unique role. For more information, see: https://www.education.govt.nz/our-work/legislation/wananga-sector-framework/.

The framework is intended to foster a flourishing wānanga sector that normalises te reo and mātauranga Māori and strengthens a sense of identity and culture for Māori learners. It is expected that this will, in turn, lead  to  associated  wellbeing  impacts  and  higher  learning  outcomes  for  Māori  learners.  This  significant reform will allow for the transfer of some governance and accountability arrangements currently held by the Crown to the founding iwi or hapū of a wānanga or another Māori organisation.

### Monitoring

The  Ministry  of  Education  monitors  three  Crown  entities:  Education  New  Zealand,  the  New  Zealand Qualifications Authority, and the TEC. This monitoring role is mandated through the Crown Entities Act 2004.

### Education Review Office

ERO is the New Zealand government's external education evaluation agency. ERO evaluate and report on the education and care of learners in schools and early childhood services.

The Chief Executive, who is also the Chief Review Officer (CRO), assigns approximately 135 review officers to undertake reviews across the country. The CRO's functions and powers are described in the Education and Training Act 2020, Part 5, subpart 3 and Part 6, subpart 6. The Act gives the CRO the power to initiate reviews, report and publish findings on the provision of education and the care for children and young people in New Zealand. This takes place in Māori-medium, English-medium and Pacific-medium contexts through:

- Regular institutional reviews

- A significant programme on system-wide inquiry, evaluation and research.

ERO undertakes regular institutional reviews. Reviews of schools are focused on continuous improvement through strengthening the capability of schools by identifying priority areas for improvement, including the effectiveness of engagement with wider communities. Reviews in early learning services are focused on accountability (including compliance with regulatory requirements), education improvement, and knowledge generation (where education is translated into achievement).

ERO conducts research and evaluations on the performance of the education system with the purpose of providing unbiased evidence to the education sector. These findings can be shared and used as a catalyst for change in achieving participation, engagement, and achievement for all learners.

ERO's  focus  areas  are  wide  ranging,  including  the  evaluation  of  groups  of  students  such  high  priority learners, key aspects in the provision in the New Zealand curriculum, and key issues affecting learners such as bullying  and  learner  wellbeing.  National  evaluations  capture  the  voice  of  children  and  young  people through student surveys, focus groups or interviews, and student perspectives from institutional reviews. ERO uses its findings and the information it gathers to remain well-informed about what's happening at a system level and the effectiveness of programmes and investments that make the biggest difference to learner outcomes. For more information, see: https://ero.govt.nz/.

### Tertiary Education Commission (TEC)

The  TEC  is  a  Crown  entity  established  under  section  159C  of  the  Education  Act  1989  and  continued under section  401  of  the  Education  and  Training  Act  2020.  TEC  monitor  the  performance  of  tertiary education organisations and report the results to the Minister of Education. TEC operate a continuum of monitoring and investigation activities that respond to the level of risk and provide guidance to the sector to help  strengthen  performance.  Intelligence  from  all  monitoring  work  informs  advice  to  the  Minister  of Education and other agencies on the performance of the tertiary education system more broadly.

TEC use audits and investigations to monitor individual tertiary education organisations and help them to address specific issues. They also undertake reviews of system-level trends and issues and share findings with the sector to help tertiary education organisations improve their performance and compliance.

The TEC also has specific functions relating to the monitoring of tertiary education institutions, the financial performance of the tertiary sector, and supporting their governance. This is to ensure the public can have confidence in the stability of the tertiary education sector in New Zealand and to minimise risks to the Crown. For more information, see: https://www.tec.govt.nz/funding/funding-andperformance/monitoring-tertiary-education-sector/monitoring-the-tertiary-education-sector-2/.

### New Zealand Qualifications Authority

NZQA are a statutory entity under Subpart 2 of Part 5 of the Education and Training Act 2020, and a Crown agent under the Crown Entities Act 2004. NZQA have authority to make rules and regulate parts of the education sector. Their purpose is to support learners in New Zealand by:

- Making sure they have access to quality training and can learn their whole lives

- Keeping complete records of their educational achievements

- Letting them take their achievements with them anywhere in the world

- Setting them up for success through our resources and services.

The  design  of  New  Zealand's  education  system  (where  schools  largely  operate  as  autonomous,  selfmanaging entities) works well for many schools, but is not without its challenges. The highly devolved system of  school  governance  empowers  schools  with  greater  flexibility  to  be  responsive  to  diverse  needs  and aspirations of the communities they serve. However, schools are largely left to operate on their own, often with slow and uneven transfers of professional knowledge, skills, and best practice, as well as duplication of effort. This has resulted in wide variability in learners' outcomes, both across and within schools. Despite a solid school governance support system, the burden on school boards and principals is high. The nature of the relationship between schools and central government can also be highly variable, and there is an opportunity for the system to be more cohesive and connected across all levels of the system.

While these challenges do exist, however, New Zealand has an extensive work programme underway to strengthen the education system, from early childhood education through to  higher education, including work to overcome challenges in the schooling system (and its governance).

Norway is a State Party to the UNESCO 1960 Convention and Recommendations against Discrimination in Education. The Convention was ratified in 1963. UNESCO's 1960 convention on its own is not specifically incorporated into national legislation. The Convention has been made directly applicable by incorporation in Norwegian domestic legal instruments. By the Storting (Norwegian parliament) decision of 13 May 2014, the Constitution of Norway was supplemented with a number of human rights provisions. The principles of equality and non-discrimination were incorporated into Article 98 of the Constitution.

The Equality and Anti-discrimination Ombud is an independent body that promotes equality and prevents discrimination in all areas of society. The Ombud provides guidance on the anti- discrimination legislation and ensures that Norwegian legislation and administrative practice are consistent with the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination and the Convention of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. Complaints concerning breaches of the legislation are dealt with by the Anti-Discrimination Tribunal. The mandate of the Ombud is stipulated under the Norwegian Anti-Discrimination Ombud Act:

Act relating to equality and a prohibition against discrimination (Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act) (Link: https://lovdata.no/dokument/NLE/lov/2017-06-16-51 )

The purpose of this Act is to promote equality and prevent discrimination on the basis of gender, pregnancy, leave in connection with childbirth or adoption, care responsibilities, ethnicity, religion, belief, disability, sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression, age or other significant characteristics of a person. This Act has the particular objective of improving the position of women and minorities. This Act shall help to dismantle disabling barriers created by society and prevent new ones from being created.

The Act applies to all sectors in Norwegian society, including education and training.

Act relating to kindergartens (the Kindergarten Act)

(Link: https://lovdata.no/dokument/NLE/lov/2005-06-17-64/KAPITTEL_5#KAPITTEL_5)

The Purpose clause in the Kindergarten Act states that "The kindergarten shall promote democracy and equality and counteract all forms of discrimination" (Section 1). Kindergartens shall "help to ensure social equality" and must strive to promote "gender equality in their educational practice. In their daily operation the institutions shall take account of children's age, level of functioning, gender, and social, ethnic, and cultural background, including the language and culture of Sami children (Section 2 – 4).

Section 16 gives all children of one year of age right to a place in a kindergarten. Children who reach the age of one by the end of August in the year of the application for a place in a kindergarten, are entitled to a place from that August. Children who reach the age of one in September, October, or November in the year of the application, are entitled to a place by the end of the month they reach the age of one. Participation in a kindergarten is voluntary, but 93.4 per cent of children aged 1-5 and 97.4 per cent of all 5-year-olds attended in 2022.

Act relating to Primary and Secondary Education (the Education Act)

(Link (only available in Norwegian): https://lovdata.no/dokument/NL/lov/2023-06-09-30?q=oppl%C3%A6ringslova ) The Purpose Clause in section 1-3 states that the education and training, amongst other purposes, shall 'provide insight into cultural diversity and show respect for the individual's convictions. […]', and that 'All forms of discrimination shall be combated.' According to section 2-1, children and young people are obliged to attend primary and lower secondary education, and have the right to a public primary and lower secondary education. Every person that has completed ten years of compulsory education is entitled by law to three years of upper secondary education and training according to section 5-1. Primary and Secondary Education is supplied free of charge.

The school has according to section 11-1 a duty to ensure that the education and training is adapted to the pupil's abilities and aptitudes. Section 11-4 –11-6 gives pupils who needs this the right to necessary physical adaptation and technical aids, personal assistance and individually tailored training.

Act relating to universites and university colleges

(Link (only available in Norwegian): https://lovdata.no/dokument/NL/lov/2024-03-08-9 )

The Act relating to universities and university colleges guarantees equal access to higher education. Student admission is regulated in chapter 8 of this Act, in order to ensure a fair admission process. Section 2-5 guarantees that higher education at state institutions is free of charge, with certain exeptions. Student from outside the European Economc Area and Switzerland need to pay tuition fees in Norway according to section 2-6, with exceptions.

According to section 10-1 of the Act, higher education institutions (HEI) are obliged to ensure that the students' learning environment is satisfactory on the basis of an overall assessment of considerations regarding the health, safety and welfare of the students. The institution must have a learning environment committee to assist in ensuring implementation. The committee must take part in the planning of measures relating to the learning environment and closely follow developments in matters concerning the safety and learning environment of the students.

As mentioned above, theprohibition of non-discrimination is regulated in the Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act. Certain aspects are, however, regulated more in detail specifically for higher education. The most important approach to ensure inclusion is the requirement for universal design of the HEI's as regulated in the Act section 10-2. This includes the HEI's main functions, such as physical environment, and information and communication technology, including the digital learning environment. Exception for this obligation relates to disproportional burden for the institutions, regulated by the Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act, sections 17 and 18.

In addition, the Act relating to universities and university colleges contains a specific obligation in section 10-3 for the institutions' board to work to prevent and stop harassment and sexual harassment, cf. section 13 of the Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act. Students with a disability and students with special needs are entitled to suitable individual adaptation of the learning environment, teaching, teaching materials and examinations, in order to ensure equal training and education opportunities, according to the Act relating to universities and university colleges, section 10-5. According to the Act relating to universities and university colleges, section 10-7, students are also ensured a right to parental leave and to resume their study program at the appropriate stage after the leave .

The State Educational Loan Fund allocates grants and loans to pupils and students according to an official cost of living estimate, stipulated annually. The support is meant to cover expenses for accommodation, subsistence costs and study materials. Means testing of the parents' economy was disbanded in the early 1970s.

Students with special needs can apply for additional support. There are special arrangements for maternity leave, illness and students with children. The size of the grant also depends on the income of the student and the income of spouse or cohabitant. Loans are interest free during the studies. In cases of low income, unemployment, illness, childbirth or care of small children,

repayments may be postponed for a period and the interest can be waived. All, or parts of the loan, may also be cancelled if illness prevents the person from working.

Norway has recently introduced a major curriculum reform for primary and upper secondary education and training. The new core curriculum was introduced in 2017 and the subject curriculum, the Knowledge Promotion 2020, was implemented from the school year 2020/21.

The aim is to facilitate better learning for all, but also to better integrate generic competencies such as critical thinking and democratic citizenship. Education is about learning and about changes and preparing for the future. A whole-school approach shall promote learning and promote common values that are the foundation of our society. These are pivotal to respond to the major challenges we are facing in Norway, but also internationally. Therefore, the new curriculum emphasizes both the importance of certain values as well as deep learning and the ability to develop key competencies in different subjects in a balanced way.

The new core curriculum, Core curriculum - values and principles for primary and upper secondary education and training, was published in September 2017, and is a part of the framework for the renewal of the school subjects. Key elements in the core curriculum are e.g. human rights, democratic citizenship, inclusion and cultural awareness. Three new cross-curricular topics will be integrated in the subjects: Sustainable development, democracy and citizenship, and health and life skills. These topics are interdisciplinary-related and will be reflected in all subjects and in all grades where appropriate.

Decentralized and Flexible Education

Decentralized and flexible education is one of the Governments main priorities, to ensure that high quality education is made available for the whole population, independent of where you live or your background. The professions educations have been prioritized within this effort to strenghten decentralized and flexible education. The Directorate of higher education and skills (HK-dir) have managed a competition-based funding programme. In 2023, the Directorate announced a call for action for at total of 200 million Norwegian kroner (NOK) for flexible and decentralized education that the educational institutions and study centers could apply for. The education offers are spread across the whole country according to local, regional and national needs, and includes between other professional educations, and educations directed towards technology, digitization and the green transition. From 2024, the competition-based funds have been moved directly to the university colleges and universities as part of their main framework allocation, funded through the Ministry of Education and Research.

Chapter 8 (Section 41-43) of the Kindergarten Act concerns the psychosocial kindergarten environment. Section 41 states that kindergartens must not accept offensie acts such as exclusion, bullying, violence, discrimination and harassment. The kondergarten staff must intervene if a child in the kindergarten is exposed to such offensive acts. The kindergarten has a duty to actively make continuous and systematic efforts to promote the health, well-being, play and learning of the children. Section 42 gives the kindergarten staff a duty to take action to ensure that the children have a safe and beneficial psychosocial kindergarten environment.

Chapter 12 (Section 12-1-12-10) of the Education Act concerns the pupils' psychosocial school environment. Section 12-2 states that all pupils have the right to a safe and beneficial school environment that promotes health, inclusion, well-being and learning. According to section 12-3, the school must not accept offensie acts such as bullying, violence, discrimination and harassment. The school must work continuously to ensure that all pupils have a safe and benedicial school environment. The school staff must intervene if a pupil is exposed to such offensive acts, and section 12-4 gives the school staff a duty to take action to ensure that the pupils feel safe and content at school. If a pupil or the pupil's parents believe that the school is not doing enough to fulfil the pupil's right to a safe and beneficial school environment, they can report the matter to the county governor according to section 12-6.

Consequences of COVID-19

-	In the Spring of 2020, the Government implemented extensive measures to combat the spread of COVID-19. The measures had a major impact for all children and young people, with the closure of kindergartens, schools and recreational activities, and with reduced municipal services. The Government stated early on that priority would be given to children and young people, and that services for children and young people would be shielded as far as was possible and in compliance with the infection control guidelines.

-	During the pandemic, the Government presented various packages with compensatory measures for children and young people, including measures to increase participation in holiday and recreational activities, measures to catch up on missed learning, and measures to strengthen mental health and prevent substance abuse problems.

-	The Coronavirus Commission and the Coronavirus Special Committee both concluded that the infection control measures were particularly hard for children and young people. A report from the Norwegian Directorate for Education (2022) concludes that the pandemic had consequences for pupils' motivation, academic progress and learning.

-	Between 2020 and 2022, the Government allocated a total of NOK 621 million for compensatory measures in the municipalities to mitigate the negative effects of the pandemic on learning and social development in children and young people.

A research group that has looked at the long-term consequences of the corona pandemic for pupils at lower secondary school has presented a report in 2024 (Subreport 2: The long-term consequences of the corona pandemic for pupils at lower secondary school, Vennerød-Diesen et al. NIFU, 2024) with the following main findings:

- Digital home school weakened the relationship between teacher and pupil.

- The pandemic intensified social exclusion.

- Pupils were given a lot of responsibility for their own learning during the pandemic.

- Vulnerable pupils have been more negatively affected by the pandemic than other pupils.

The pandemic reinforced already existing differences among pupils, and vulnerable pupils were particularly affected. Lower secondary school teachers express concern about the pupils' academic progress, especially in Norwegian and mathematics, and the fact that pupils experienced a loss of learning.

Temporary legislative measures during the pandemic

Temporary Act of 26 May 2020 No. 52 on adaptations in the Kindergarten Act, the Education Act and the Independent School Act to remedy the consequences of the outbreak of covid-19, provided temporary regulations in the area of kindergartens and schools during the pandemic. The purpose of the law was that children, youth and adults should have their rights safeguarded to the greatest extent possible under the Kindergartens Act, the Education Act and the Independent School Act, while taking into account what was possible when kindergartens and schools were closed or subject to restrictions of infection control reasons as a result of the outbreak of covid-19. Among other things, the Act provided the opportunity to make adjustments to the rules on the right to a place in kindergarten and facilitation during periods when the kindergartens were closed or operated with restrictions as a result of measures based on the Infection Control Act. As for schools, the Act established that school owners have a duty to provide education as far as possible, even if the schools are closed or operating with restrictions pursuant to individual decisions or regulations based on the Infection Control Act. Moreover, regulations were provided on the application of the Education Act and the Independent School Act during periods when schools were closed or operated with restrictions as a result of measures based on the Infection Control Act. The law was repealed on 31 December 2022.

Individual adoptation

In Norway, the assessment of individual adoptation needs takes place at the universities and university colleges, and the assessment must be in line with the provisions of the Act relating to universities and university colleges and the Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act. Individual adoptation shall be assessed by the individual institution, and there are no national regulation stipulating what kind of individual adoptation the institutions shall provide. Adequate knowledge and expertise at the institutions concerning individual adoptations is therefore important. As the responsibility lies with the institutions, different assessments and different practices may arise in individual cases. The Directorate for Higher Education and Skills (HK-dir) can provide guidance to the institutions on individual adaptation, universal design and learning environment. In addition to this, universities and university colleges shall ensure that all students have access to a Student Ombudsman (Studentombud), who can provide students with advice and assistance in matters related to their study situation, and contribute to safeguarding the students' legal rights. The Student Ombudsman cannot be instructed in his or her work, and can decide whether to assist a student in a given case. The Student Ombudsman does not have the authority to decide individual cases.

Statistics

In 2023, around 21 per cent of pupils in Norwegian primary schools had an immigrant background, and 41 per cent of these had immigrated themselves. 21.4 per cent of the students in upper secondary schools had an immigrant background, and 71 per cent of these had immigrated themselves. Immigrant students, especially those who arrive in Norway as teenagers, face tougher challenges than other students in achieving good results from their education.

Legislation provides for their right to education

Children from families seeking asylum do not have a right to a kindergarten place until their asylum application has been accepted and the family has a permanent address in a municipality. However, children staying in an asylum centre may enter a kindergarten if places are available in the municipality where the centre is located. A government grant finances full time places for all children in asylum centres aged one to five regardless of the status of their asylum application. For the municipalities to be better able to respond to the current refugee situation due to the war in Ukraine, some temporary legislative changes of the Kindergarten Act, making it easier to establish contemporary kindergartens, have been implemented. According to the Education Act section 2-1, the right to primary and lower secondary education applies when it is probable that the child will reside in Norway for a period of more than three months. The obligation to attend primary and lower secondary education commences as soon as the presence has lasted for three months. These rules apply to every child, including children of asylum seekers, unaccompanied minors seeking asylum and irregular immigrants. For the municipalities to be better able to respond to the current refugee situation due to the war in Ukraine, the Government suggested some temporary legislative changes, which the Storting approved. The temporary changes have been further extended. The most important temporary change of the Education Act has been that all children have the right to school as soon as possible and no later than within three months after arrival. Normally the rule is within one month. All children are obliged to go to school when they have been in the country for three months. The temporary legislation means that the municipalities have more time to prepare and provide fulltime education according to the Education Act. However, they have to offer the best possible education as soon as possible. Youth who have completed lower secondary school in Norway or a comparable education in another country, have the right to upper secondary education according to section 5-1 of the Education Act. This also applies to those who have completed further education in another country, but do not get the training approved as a study qualification or vocational qualification in Norway. The right only applies to those who are likely to be in Norway for more than three months.  It is a condition for the right to upper secondary education that the applicant has legal residence in Norway. According to section 5-9 of the Education Act, persons who reside legally in the country while they are waiting for a response to their application for a residence permit have the right to upper secondary education for the school year leading up to the year they turn 18. Those whose application for a residence permit is rejected only have the right to further education until the date of the final decision. The application deadline for admission to upper secondary school is February 1 or March 1 each year, but the County Authority must also take into consideration applications they receive after the deadline. Some counties provided activities for youth from Ukraine shortly after they started arriving in Norway during spring 2022. All of them continue to provide education as applied for or other kinds of introductory offers. According to the Education Act section 9-7, students who have the right to upper secondary education can be given more primary/ lower secondary education before or in combination with upper secondary education. This will help students who need more preparatory education to benefit from upper secondary education. This is especially relevant for students arriving in Norway late in their schooling and have limited time before they enter upper secondary school. From 2020, the funds for the government scheme (The Job Opportunity Program Part B) to support school owners who organise such education were transferred to the county municipality. According to the Education Act section 3-6 and section 6-5, a pupil attending the primary, lower, and upper secondary education and who has a mother tongue other than Norwegian or Sami, has the right to adapted education in Norwegian until they are sufficiently proficient in Norwegian to attend the regular instruction offered. If necessary, such pupils are also entitled to mother tongue instruction, bilingual subject teaching, or both. In 2022-2023, almost 44 000 pupils in primary and lower secondary schools received adapted education in Norwegian.

Early Childhood Education and Care (ECEC) is a non-obligatory part of the education system in Norway. ECEC is provided by kindergartens. These are pedagogical institutions that cater for children from 0 to 5 years of age. Some 50 % of the kindergartens are privately owned and operated with public funding, based on formal approval by the authorities. Since 2009 all children, irrespective of their background, have a legal right to a place in kindergarten from 1 year of age 2.

Nearly all children in Norway have been to Kindergarten before entering primary school. The participation rate for all three, four-and five-year olds is 97.2 %. A high proportion of children less than 3 years of age also have a place in a kindergarten (88.6 %). For minority language speaking children, the participation rate is a little lower than for the age cohort as a whole. This also holds true for the three, four- and five-year olds, but the gap is more evident for younger minority children. 94.7 % of minority languages speaking 5-year olds now attend kindergarten before entering school. However, participation rates for minority languages speaking children have increased also for the youngest children. Participation of minority language speaking children aged 1-2 years has risen from 69.1 % in 2019 to 75 % in 2023.

Learning outcomes (see similar text provided for under secondary education)

There has been a negative development in learning outcomes in recent years.

Norwegian pupils have participated in international surveys since the mid-1990s. Surveys such as TIMSS, PISA and PIRLS make it possible to see the development of pupils learning results over time, and to compare between countries.

The development in results from the international surveys in the period 1995-2015 was characterized by a decline followed by an improvement.  The positive development was largely linked to an improvement in results for the lowest performing pupils. The progress was also most marked for mathematics and science at the 4th grade. PIRLS 2016 showed that Norwegian 10-year-olds were far above the international average in reading, and that Norwegian pupils had made clear progress since PIRLS 2006.

From 2015, there are signs of decline in several surveys. In PISA 2018, the Norwegian 15-year-olds performed more poorly in science and reading compared to PISA 2015. That year there was also a clear change in the pupils' reading habits compared to PISA 2009. Several pupils said that they spent less time reading in their free time. At the same time, the time they spent reading on the screen increased.

TIMSS 2019 showed consistently good results in mathematics and science at the primary level, but a significant decrease in the results at the lower secondary level.  PIRLS 2021 showed for the first time a decline in 10-year-olds' reading skills. The results were still well above the international average. Norwegian 10-year-olds reported the lowest reading enjoyment of all the countries in the survey.  PIRLS 2021 also showed a marked increase in the use of digital devices in schools from 2016-2021.

PISA 2022 showed a decline in reading, mathematics and science compared to 2018. The decline was large in mathematics and reading, and somewhat less in science. There was also a significant increase in pupils at the lowest mastery levels. The OECD average decreased in all subject areas, compared to 2018. But the decrease in Norway was greater than in most other OECD countries.

In addition to seeing an average negative development in learning results, PISA 2022 showed a sharp increase in the proportion of pupils at the lowest mastery levels. Overall, 41 per cent of the pupils performed at the lowest mastery levels in at least one or more of the subject areas. These pupils will be poorly prepared for further education and working life. PISA 2022 also showed that the proportion of pupils at the highest levels decreased somewhat in reading, and significantly decreased in mathematics from the previous survey.

A selection of the Norwegian 15-year-olds who took part in PISA 2022 also answered tasks and questions about personal finances (Financial Literacy). This section shows that there are large differences in Norwegian pupils' knowledge of personal finances and financial concepts. The Norwegian pupils score just below the OECD average, and over one in five pupils perform at the lowest levels.

In addition to the surveys that measure reading, science and mathematics, Norwegian pupils also participate in surveys that provide information on the development of knowledge about democracy and citizenship (ICCS). The Norwegian pupils showed good knowledge and skills compared to other countries that participated in the ICCS surveys in 2009 and 2016. ICCS 2022 showed for the first time a decline in pupils' knowledge of democracy. The results on the knowledge test were lower in 2022 than in 2009. Norwegian pupils were still above the international average in the survey but had the largest decline of all participating countries.

There are gender differences in learning outcomes and well-being. Various surveys and statistics show that Norwegian girls have a higher learning outcome than boys in several areas at school. At the end of primary school, girls do better than boys in exams. The girls also get better grades in all subjects except physical education and achieve on average four primary school credits more than the boys. This difference is somewhat smaller than for the pandemic years. At the same time, girls score the highest on several negative developmental traits. Girls have worse well-being at school than boys, have more absence from school, and have more psychological problems.

We are experiencing increasing challenges in the school environment. A negative development in the learning environment also emerges in several of the international surveys. Analyzes of the TIMSS results show a negative development in learning focus, school affiliation, safe school environment and bullying from 2015 to 2019 among Norwegian pupils in the 9th grade.  The PIRLS survey shows that more 5th graders report bullying in 2021 than in 2016.

The Government believes that more practical, active, and varied learning in schools are the most important measures to reverse the negative development in learning outcomes and well-being. In September 2024, the Government therefore presented a white paper to Parliament in which these topics were dealt with.

Learning outcomes (see similar text provided for under primary education)

There has been a negative development in learning outcomes in recent years.

Norwegian pupils have participated in international surveys since the mid-1990s. Surveys such as TIMSS, PISA and PIRLS make it possible to see the development of pupils learning results over time, and to compare between countries.

The development in results from the international surveys in the period 1995-2015 was characterized by a decline followed by an improvement.  The positive development was largely linked to an improvement in results for the lowest performing pupils. The progress was also most marked for mathematics and science at the 4th grade. PIRLS 2016 showed that Norwegian 10-year-olds were far above the international average in reading, and that Norwegian pupils had made clear progress since PIRLS 2006.

From 2015, there are signs of decline in several surveys. In PISA 2018, the Norwegian 15-year-olds performed more poorly in science and reading compared to PISA 2015. That year there was also a clear change in the pupils' reading habits compared to PISA 2009. Several pupils said that they spent less time reading in their free time. At the same time, the time they spent reading on the screen increased.

TIMSS 2019 showed consistently good results in mathematics and science at the primary level, but a significant decrease in the results at the lower secondary level.  PIRLS 2021 showed for the first time a decline in 10-year-olds' reading skills. The results were still well above the international average. Norwegian 10-year-olds reported the lowest reading enjoyment of all the countries in the survey.  PIRLS 2021 also showed a marked increase in the use of digital devices in schools from 2016-2021.

PISA 2022 showed a decline in reading, mathematics and science compared to 2018. The decline was large in mathematics and reading, and somewhat less in science. There was also a significant increase in pupils at the lowest mastery levels. The OECD average decreased in all subject areas, compared to 2018. But the decrease in Norway was greater than in most other OECD countries.

In addition to seeing an average negative development in learning results, PISA 2022 showed a sharp increase in the proportion of pupils at the lowest mastery levels. Overall, 41 per cent of the pupils performed at the lowest mastery levels in at least one or more of the subject areas. These pupils will be poorly prepared for further education and working life. PISA 2022 also showed that the proportion of pupils at the highest levels decreased somewhat in reading, and significantly decreased in mathematics from the previous survey.

A selection of the Norwegian 15-year-olds who took part in PISA 2022 also answered tasks and questions about personal finances (Financial Literacy). This section shows that there are large differences in Norwegian pupils' knowledge of personal finances and financial concepts. The Norwegian pupils score just below the OECD average, and over one in five pupils perform at the lowest levels.

In addition to the surveys that measure reading, science and mathematics, Norwegian pupils also participate in surveys that provide information on the development of knowledge about democracy and citizenship (ICCS). The Norwegian pupils showed good knowledge and skills compared to other countries that participated in the ICCS surveys in 2009 and 2016. ICCS 2022 showed for the first time a decline in pupils' knowledge of democracy. The results on the knowledge test were lower in 2022 than in 2009. Norwegian pupils were still above the international average in the survey but had the largest decline of all participating countries.

Features of higher education in Norway

In the course of a few decades, higher education has gone from being reserved for the few to becoming available to a higher proportion of the population. It is a goal that everyone should be able to study regardless of social and geographical background and that the student population reflects the population as a whole as much as possible. Since the turn of the millennium, there has been significant growth in the Norwegian student population, from 186,000 in 2000 to 299,000 students in 2023. A distinctive feature of Norwegian higher education is the wide spread in age from the youngest to the oldest students. This contributes to a diverse student population. Social background is still important for whom takes part in higher education. Young people with highly educated parents take part in higher education to a greater extent than young people who do not have parents with a higher education. Especially on studies that require a high grade point average for entry, a larger proportion of students have parents with higher education. In addition, Norwegian-born with immigrant parents have a markedly higher propensity to study than both immigrants and the rest of the population. Moreover, higher education is characterized by the underrepresentation of men. Numbers show that two out of five students are male and that the distribution has been fairly stable over the past 20 years.

Admission to higher education

Some study programmes  require specific subjects from upper secondary education and/or practice, and there are several ways to enter higher education. Acceptance to higher education requires a Diploma from upper secondary school with general admission requirements. This is also awarded to non-vocational students or vocational students who choose to not take their apprenticeship, or who choose to study the required competency courses in upper secondary school. Conversely, study competence can be achieved by the so-called 23/5 rule where applicants must be 23 years of age and have a total of five years of education and/or work experience, as well as passed courses in Norwegian, English, mathematics, science and social studies. To be qualified for certain study programmes (for instance engineering or medical school), the applicant must cover advanced courses in science, such as mathematics, physics and chemistry. Admittance to bachelor level programs is coordinated through the Norwegian Universities and Colleges Admission Service based on grade point average from upper secondary education, with the highest-ranking students offered a place. Additional points are awarded students with science or language specialization from upper secondary education. The number of points awarded also depends on the students age, and whether the student have completed one year of higher education, military service, folk high school or vocational training. Secondary school grades can later be improved via the arrangement of re-sit exams, but half of the study places are only offered based on original transcripts without age or study points. A student at a university or a university college can get scholarships and/or loans from The State Educational Loan Fund. A student welfare organization shall, in cooperation with education institutions, offer student welfare, such as student housing. Several government initiated processes have taken place since 2021 in terms of assessing and reviewing the admission system for higher education. In 2021, the Government appointed a public committee to examine the regulations for admission to higher education. The committee presented an Offical Norwegian Report (green paper) in December 2022. Following up the report, the Government presented a White Paper in 2024 to the Parliament with proposed changes to the admission system The proposed changes are aimed at ensuring that students commence their studies at a younger age than today, enable participation in the labour market at an earlier stage and ensure adequate recruitment to important professions. Some of the proposed changes include lowering the amount of additional points an applicant can attain, replacing gender points with gender quotas and discontinuing specific subject requirements for bachelor degrees in nursing. Furthermore, the changes should simplify current regulations. The Parliament reviewed the White Paper in the Spring of 2024 and adopted a resolution in support of the proposed changes. The changes may be implemented at different stages and most changes will at the earliest stage enter into force from 2027, to ensure predictability for future applicants, Legislative changes are required before the changes can enter into force.

Tuition fees for degree students from outside of the EU and EFTA

Higher education is free of charge for Norwegian citizens and citizens of the EEA. Until 2023, public higher education institutions in Norway have not charged tuition fees for ordinary study programs. In 2023, the Government proposed that from the fall semester of 2023, universities and colleges should charge at least cost-covering tuition fees from new degree students from outside the EU and EFTA. The proposal for tuition fees was passed by the Parliament and from the fall semester of 2023, degree students from countries outside of the EU and EFTA are required a tuition fee. The necessary legislative changes were adopted by the Storting in June 2023. Several exemptions to the requirement apply, such as for exchange students, joint-degree students, PhD students, refugees and foreign citizens with equal rights as Norwegian citizens. In 2024, the Government decided to establish a scholarship program for foreign degree students from chosen partner countries in the Global South, entitled NORSTIP. The program will provide scholarships for approximately 200 students, covering costs related to both tuition fee and living costs. The first scholarships were provided for the fall semester of 2024, and the program is administered by the Directorate for Higher Education and Training (HK-dir). As for the financing of the county and municipal government, the Norwegian Parliament has adopted a decentralised administrative structure, which delegates considerable authority and financial freedom of action to the county level. Block grants are given, and county and municipal authorities determine their activities according to existing legislation and regulations.

The Ministry of Education and Research emphasises the importance of placing local responsibility for didactical interpretation and adaptation with the school owner, in accordance with the Education Act and national regulations. All state institutions are expected to use a system of planning that covers both the short term (the budget year) and the medium term (3-4 years or more). State institutions must also formulate their objectives through a dialogue with the responsible Ministry and establish a system for following up their results.

The administration and management of schools and institutions vary according to their level in the education system. The municipality/county administration influences the extent of self-governance in schools/institutions in the municipality/county. In higher education, all institutions are administered by the Ministry of Education and Research and follow the provisions laid down in Act of 1 August 2024 on Universities and University Colleges, as well as the general laws, agreements and provisions applicable to all state institutions. The Ministry is also responsible for monitoring private higher education.

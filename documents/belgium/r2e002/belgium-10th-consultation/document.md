The Flemish education system strives towards an inclusive educational system, based on equal opportunities, which is accompanied by a transversal monitoring system. On September 4, 2015 The Flemish Government voiced the consensus that under no condition the influx of refugees could function as an excuse not to fully grant the fundamental rights to which each and every person is entitled when on Belgian territory. They therefore established an Inter-Ministerial Committee for the Refugee and Asylum Crisis that was entrusted with overseeing coordinated action of all policy domains in cooperation with civil society. For education, as for the other policy domains, a 2 track strategy was followed: 1. Make full use of the available provisions and give full (budgetary) implementation of the established legislation 2. Take extra measures to fill the gaps in provision and/or to guarantee the sustainability of the support measure. This strategy was followed by the Flemish Parliament that adopted the necessary budget allocations for the implementation. Extra measures in order to tackle the refugee crisis Apart from the general legislation that provides extra budget to schools based on the presence of pupils from socially disadvantaged families or with special educational needs, for the refugee children the right to access to a place at school was applied rigorously and within the given delays of max. 60 days upon arrival in Belgium. During the transitory period of max. 60 days, temporary provision of education can be given in or close to the refugee centres themselves. Moreover the framework for support to newly arrived immigrant children who don't master the (Dutch) instruction language was applied strictly, with automatic granting of extra teaching hours in primary education and special welcoming classes in secondary schools. In the cities with school capacity problems, the local consultation platforms were entrusted with their regular task to draw up a plan to attribute each refugee child a place at school while respecting the legally defined principle of maintaining a good social mix at school and avoiding segregation. Soon it appeared that the normal legal framework was not sufficient and urgent measures were taken in addition: 1. Extra (temporary) welcoming classes to bridge the period between arrival and the provision of a final study place at school In contrast with our neighbouring countries, Belgium has showed an explicit concern to avoid situations where children have to follow courses in the asylum centre itself. In the few cases where this was necessary, the temporary education offer functioned as an emergency measure, and the education provision was functioning as an extra campus of a nearby school. In any case the deadline of maximum 60 days applies but it remains the overall policy aim is to have the refugee children registered as soon as possible in a regular school, even before the legally stipulated 60 days. This required extra welcoming classes in schools to be set up rapidly. 2. Extra hours for linguistic integration for children in pre-primary education (not only primary and secondary) Schools with non-Dutch speaking children younger than age 5 received € 950 for each student as counted on 01/02/2016 for (language) learning support. Moreover extra teaching hours were provided as the schoolyear progressed in case the number of non-Dutch speaking children increased. 3. Extra funding for transport (from refugee centre to school and vice versa in order to make use of available places at schools located further away or out of the crowded city centres) Elementary education: in case of asylum centres with difficult access to school by public transport, schools were given financial support for organising coach transport between the school and the asylum centre. In the case of secondary education: the provision for extra transport is usually provided by the asylum centre and in some cases supported by the social services of the municipality concerned. ADULT EDUCATION Here a solid framework existed with widespread provision for linguistic integration of adult migrants. Moreover unemployed migrants are

[[END OF PAGE 47]]

[[START OF PAGE 48]]

bound to follow a 'integration track' combining language courses, civic/societal training and preparation for work. In this context different approaches exist in language courses depending on the preparation of the learner, e.g. illiterate migrants will follow another track than migrants with a higher education degree with already a good account of English or another European language. Refugees have been systematically guided towards these differentiated learning tracks for adult migrants and the budgets for this type of education provision were adjusted according to the increasing number of learners. Additional coaching measures remained, however, necessary to accommodate the situation in which refugees start their learning path on one location - mostly close to the refugee centre or the first place of residence - in case of removal. As many immigrants refugees show indeed preference to eventually settle in larger cities for professional reasons or in order to be closer to members of their own cultural community. It is important that the coaching can go on without interruption in the new place of residence. HIGHER EDUCATION Since the 1990-ies the Flemish Community of Belgium had specific legislation on refugees students in relation to i.a. access to higher education and financial support during their studies. Building on this already fully established legislative framework, universities and university colleges accommodated the newly arrived students. Moreover, according to a recent survey conducted by the Council of Europe, Belgium is one of the few countries that systematically applies the protocol on the recognition of higher education qualifications of refugees and persons with refugee-like status of the COE/UNESCO/EU Lisbon Recognition Convention. On this basis refugees can easily apply for recognition of their qualifications, including in situations where the qualification documents are lost or unavailable. As a supplementary measure at the level of the Government administration, extra staff for the National Academic Recognition Centre was appointed and specific training organized in order to accommodate the increase of academic recognition files introduced by refugees.
